Common Fractures 51 questions

Neck of femur

Must-doRepeated15 past questions

Intracapsular femoral neck fractures cut the blood supply (medial circumflex femoral artery): avascular necrosis and non-union. Young patients: urgent closed or open reduction and internal fixation to save the head. Elderly displaced fractures: hemiarthroplasty or total hip replacement. Leg is short and externally rotated.

Try a past question

A 35-year-old male presents with a fracture of the neck of femur (NOF) following trauma. Imaging confirms it is an intracapsular fracture. What is the best treatment option in this case?

  1. A. Closed Reduction (CR)
  2. B. Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF)
  3. C. Hemiarthroplasty (HAP)
  4. D. Total Hip Replacement

Answer: B. In a young adult the femoral head is saved by reduction and internal fixation as soon as possible.

Intertrochanteric fracture

High-yield4 past questions

Intertrochanteric fractures are extracapsular, so the blood supply is kept and they heal well (but can malunite into varus). Elderly women after a fall; marked external rotation and shortening. Fixed with a dynamic hip screw or a proximal femoral nail.

Supracondylar humerus

High-yield6 past questions

Supracondylar fracture of the humerus: the commonest elbow fracture in children, after a fall on the outstretched hand (extension type). Risks: brachial artery injury and Volkmann ischaemic contracture, median and anterior interosseous nerve injury, cubitus varus (gunstock deformity) from malunion.

Colles fracture

High-yield7 past questions

Colles fracture: lower radius within 2.5 cm of the wrist, the fragment tilted back (dinner-fork deformity); older women after a fall on the outstretched hand. Complications: malunion (the commonest), median nerve compression, rupture of extensor pollicis longus, Sudeck atrophy. Smith fracture tilts forward.

Scaphoid fracture

Must-doRepeated6 past questions

Scaphoid: the commonest carpal fracture; fall on the outstretched hand, tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox. Early X-rays can be normal: treat as a fracture and repeat X-ray (or MRI). Blood enters from the far end, so the proximal pole can die (avascular necrosis) or not unite.

Galeazzi fracture

High-yield3 past questions

Galeazzi: fracture of the radius shaft with dislocation of the lower radio-ulnar joint (needs open fixation). Monteggia: fracture of the ulna with dislocation of the radial head (watch the posterior interosseous nerve). Memory aid: 'GRUM' - Galeazzi radius, ulna Monteggia.

Arthritis 50 questions

Osteoarthritis

Must-doRepeated10 past questions

Osteoarthritis: wear of cartilage; pain worse with use, stiffness under 30 minutes. X-ray: narrowed joint space (medial in the knee, so bow legs, genu varum), osteophytes, sclerosis, cysts. Heberden nodes (DIP) and Bouchard nodes (PIP). Knee replacement for end-stage disease.

Try a past question

Which deformity is classically seen in advanced osteoarthritis of the knee?

  1. A. Genu valgum
  2. B. Genu varum
  3. C. Genu recurvatum
  4. D. Flexion contracture

Answer: B. The medial knee compartment wears first, so advanced knee osteoarthritis gives bow legs (genu varum).

Rheumatoid arthritis

Must-doRepeated6 past questions

Rheumatoid arthritis: symmetrical small joints of the hands (MCP and PIP) and feet first, morning stiffness over an hour; spares the DIP joints. Anti-CCP is most specific. Deformities: swan neck, boutonnière, ulnar deviation, Z thumb. Methotrexate is the first DMARD.

  2. B. Hip joint
  3. C. First carpometacarpal joint
  4. D. Metacarpophalangeal joints

Answer: D. Rheumatoid arthritis usually starts in the MCP and PIP joints of the hands.

Ankylosing spondylitis

Must-do11 past questions

Ankylosing spondylitis: young men, inflammatory back pain better with exercise, HLA-B27. Sacroiliitis first; bamboo spine and squaring of vertebrae later; reduced chest expansion; anterior uveitis. Schober test measures lumbar flexion. NSAIDs first, then anti-TNF drugs.

Gout and crystal arthropathies

High-yieldRepeated23 past questions

Gout: monosodium urate crystals, needle-shaped and negatively birefringent. Big toe (podagra) first. Acute attack: NSAIDs, colchicine (blocks microtubules) or steroids; never start allopurinol during an attack. Pseudogout: calcium pyrophosphate, rhomboid and positively birefringent, the knee, chondrocalcinosis.

  2. B. Colchicine
  3. C. Febuxostat
  4. D. Probenecid

Answer: B. Colchicine treats acute gout by blocking microtubule formation in neutrophils.

Fracture Principles & Trauma 46 questions

Fracture healing

Must-doRepeated4 past questions

Stages: haematoma, inflammation, soft callus, hard callus, remodelling. Healing is slowed by poor blood supply (scaphoid, femoral neck, lower tibia), movement at the fracture, infection, smoking and diabetes.

Try a past question

Which is the first stage in fracture healing?

  1. A. Hematoma formation
  2. B. Soft callus
  3. C. Hard callus
  4. D. Remodeling

Answer: A. Fracture healing begins with a haematoma at the fracture site.

Complications: delayed union, non-union, malunion

Must-do7 past questions

Delayed union, non-union (no healing after 6-9 months; hypertrophic or atrophic) and malunion. Avascular necrosis follows femoral neck, scaphoid and talus fractures. Sudeck (complex regional pain syndrome), myositis ossificans after elbow injuries, Volkmann ischaemic contracture after supracondylar fractures.

Polytrauma

Must-do4 past questions

ATLS order: airway with cervical spine control, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure. Pelvic fractures can bleed massively: bind the pelvis. Damage control orthopaedics: external fixation first in unstable patients, definitive fixation later.

Compartment syndrome

Must-doRepeated15 past questions

Pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch of the muscles, a tense swollen limb; pulses are lost late. Commonest after tibial fractures and tight casts. Split the cast and dressings, then urgent fasciotomy if pressure is over 30 mmHg (or within 30 of the diastolic).

Answer: B. Severe pain with pain on passive stretch and a tense limb after a tibial fracture is compartment syndrome.

Fat embolism syndrome

Must-doRepeated8 past questions

Fat embolism syndrome 24-72 hours after long bone (femur) fractures: breathlessness and hypoxia, confusion, and a petechial rash on the chest, armpits and eyes (Gurd criteria). Prevent by early fixation; treat with oxygen and support.

  3. C. Acute respiratory distress syndrome
  4. D. Septic shock

Answer: B. Breathlessness, petechiae and confusion a day after a long bone fracture is fat embolism syndrome.

Bone Tumors 36 questions

Osteochondroma

Must-do3 past questions

Osteochondroma (exostosis): the commonest benign bone tumour; a bony outgrowth with a cartilage cap pointing away from the joint, near the knee. Growth after skeletal maturity or a thick cap (over 2 cm) suggests change to chondrosarcoma.

Giant cell tumor

Must-doRepeated11 past questions

Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma): young adults after the growth plate closes (20-40), at the epiphysis of the lower femur, upper tibia or lower radius. X-ray: lytic, eccentric, 'soap bubble'. Locally aggressive, can go to the lungs. Treated by curettage with bone graft or cement.

Enchondroma

Must-do4 past questions

Enchondroma: a cartilage tumour inside the bones of the hand (the commonest hand bone tumour), with stippled calcification. Multiple: Ollier disease; with haemangiomas: Maffucci syndrome (higher risk of chondrosarcoma).

Osteosarcoma

Must-doRepeated7 past questions

Osteosarcoma: teenagers, the metaphysis around the knee. Sunray spicules and Codman triangle (lifted periosteum) on X-ray; raised alkaline phosphatase. Spreads to the lungs. Linked to retinoblastoma (RB gene) and Paget disease in the elderly. Chemotherapy then limb-sparing surgery.

Ewing sarcoma

Must-doRepeated4 past questions

Ewing sarcoma: children 5-15, the diaphysis of long bones and the pelvis. Fever and a raised ESR can mimic infection. Onion-peel periosteal reaction; small round blue cells, t(11;22). Very sensitive to radiotherapy.

Multiple myeloma

Must-do4 past questions

Multiple myeloma: the commonest primary bone malignancy in adults over 50. Bone pain, anaemia, kidney failure, high calcium (CRAB); punched-out lytic skull lesions; bone scan may be cold, so a skeletal survey is used. M band on electrophoresis.

Answer: A. Anaemia, bone pain, kidney problems and high calcium together point to multiple myeloma.

Metabolic Bone Disease 35 questions

Osteoporosis

Must-do13 past questions

DEXA T-score: −1 or above normal; −1 to −2.5 osteopenia; −2.5 or below osteoporosis. Fragility fractures: spine (wedge), hip, wrist (Colles). Calcium and vitamin D for all; bisphosphonates (alendronate) first-line drugs.

Try a past question

  1. A. Normal bone density
  2. B. Osteopenia
  3. C. Osteoporosis
  4. D. Severe osteoporosis only if fracture present

Answer: C. A T-score of −2.5 or lower (here −2.7) is osteoporosis.

Rickets

High-yield12 past questions

Rickets: vitamin D lack in children. Widened wrists, rachitic rosary, bow legs, Harrison sulcus, delayed fontanelle closure, frontal bossing. X-ray: cupping and fraying of the metaphysis. Blood: low calcium and phosphate, high alkaline phosphatase. In adults: osteomalacia.

High-yield4 past questions

Hyperparathyroidism in bone: subperiosteal bone loss on the radial side of the middle phalanges (the most specific X-ray sign), salt-and-pepper skull, brown tumours, osteitis fibrosa cystica. High calcium and PTH.

Joint Injuries 32 questions

Shoulder dislocation

High-yieldRepeated7 past questions

Anterior dislocation is the commonest (flattened shoulder, axillary nerve risk); Bankart and Hill-Sachs lesions cause recurrence. Posterior dislocation after seizures or electric shock: arm held internally rotated, 'light bulb' sign on X-ray. Reduction: Kocher or traction methods.

  3. C. Acromioclavicular joint dislocation
  4. D. Proximal humeral shaft fracture

Answer: B. Shoulder pain after a seizure is the classic story of posterior dislocation.

Rotator cuff injuries

High-yield4 past questions

Supraspinatus is the most often torn rotator cuff tendon. Painful arc between 60° and 120° of abduction; drop arm test for a full tear. MRI or ultrasound to confirm. Older patients often manage with physiotherapy; repair for active patients.

Knee ligaments

Must-doRepeated15 past questions

ACL tear: a twisting injury with a pop and swelling within hours (haemarthrosis); Lachman test is the most sensitive. PCL: dashboard injury, posterior sag. Medial collateral: valgus stress. Unhappy triad (O'Donoghue): ACL, MCL and medial meniscus. MRI is the investigation of choice.

  3. C. Anterior cruciate ligament
  4. D. Lateral collateral ligament

Answer: C. A twist with a pop and quick swelling is an anterior cruciate ligament tear.

Meniscal injuries

High-yield4 past questions

Meniscal tears: twisting on a bent knee; locking, clicking, joint line tenderness, swelling the next day. McMurray and Apley tests. The medial meniscus tears more often (it is fixed to the medial ligament). Bucket-handle tears lock the knee. MRI; arthroscopic repair or trimming.

Upper Limb Nerve Injuries 27 questions

Radial nerve

Must-doRepeated7 past questions

Radial nerve: injured with fractures of the humerus shaft (spiral groove) and by pressure in the arm (Saturday night palsy). Wrist drop, loss of finger and thumb extension, numb back of the first web space. Most recover; a cock-up splint meanwhile.

Try a past question

  1. A. Axillary nerve
  2. B. Radial nerve
  3. C. Ulnar nerve
  4. D. Musculocutaneous nerve

Answer: B. The radial nerve runs in the spiral groove of the humerus and is hurt in shaft fractures.

Median nerve

Must-doRepeated5 past questions

Median nerve: high lesions (supracondylar fracture) give the 'pointing index' (benediction hand on fist making) and lost thumb opposition; low lesions (carpal tunnel, wrist cuts) give an ape thumb and numb lateral three and a half fingers. Anterior interosseous branch: cannot make an 'OK' sign.

  3. C. Median nerve
  4. D. Axillary nerve

Answer: C. An index finger that will not flex in a fist, weak opposition and lateral palm numbness mean a median nerve lesion.

Ulnar nerve

Must-doRepeated8 past questions

Ulnar nerve: injured at the elbow (medial epicondyle). Claw hand (ring and little fingers), wasting of the hand's small muscles, Froment sign (thumb flexes to grip paper because adductor pollicis is weak), numb little finger. Ulnar paradox: a lower lesion gives worse clawing.

  4. D. Musculocutaneous nerve

Answer: A. Froment's sign shows weak adductor pollicis, which is supplied by the ulnar nerve.

Axillary nerve

Must-doRepeated6 past questions

Axillary nerve: injured in shoulder dislocation and fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus. Loss of deltoid (abduction from 15° to 90°) and numbness over the 'regimental badge' area of the upper arm.

Pediatric Orthopedics 21 questions

Osteogenesis imperfecta

Must-do3 past questions

Osteogenesis imperfecta: a defect in type I collagen; brittle bones with many fractures, blue sclerae, hearing loss, poor teeth. Can be mistaken for child abuse. Bisphosphonates reduce fractures.

  2. B. Hydroxylation of proline and lysine
  3. C. Glycosylation of serine residues
  4. D. Methylation of lysine

Answer: A. Recurrent fractures with blue sclerae come from a type I collagen gene mutation.

Club foot (CTEV)

Must-doRepeated5 past questions

Club foot (CTEV): ankle equinus, hindfoot varus, forefoot adduction, midfoot cavus (CAVE). Ponseti method: serial casts from the first weeks (correct cavus first), percutaneous Achilles tenotomy, then a foot abduction brace for years.

  3. C. Calcaneovalgus deformity
  4. D. Congenital talipes equinovarus

Answer: D. Forefoot adduction, hindfoot varus, equinus and cavus together are congenital talipes equinovarus.

Spine 15 questions

Disc herniation

High-yieldRepeated3 past questions

Commonest at L4-L5 and L5-S1, usually posterolateral, pressing on the lower root (L5 or S1). Straight leg raising test positive. Cauda equina syndrome (saddle numbness, bladder and bowel trouble) needs emergency surgery. MRI is the investigation of choice.

Spondylolisthesis

High-yield3 past questions

Spondylolisthesis: forward slip of one vertebra on the one below, most often L5 on S1. Spondylolysis: a defect in the pars interarticularis, 'Scottie dog with a collar' on an oblique X-ray. Graded by Meyerding by the percentage of slip.

  4. D. Ankylosing spondylitis

Answer: B. Forward displacement of L5 over S1 is spondylolisthesis.

Tuberculosis spine

Must-doRepeated7 past questions

Pott spine: the commonest site of skeletal TB, usually the thoracolumbar spine. Starts at the disc and the vertebral endplates (paradiscal), the disc space narrows, a cold abscess forms (psoas abscess), vertebrae collapse into a kyphosis (gibbus). Paraplegia can follow. MRI is the investigation of choice; anti-TB drugs, surgery for paraplegia.

  2. B. Tuberculosis of spine
  3. C. Scoliosis
  4. D. Disc prolapse

Answer: B. A gibbus (sharp angular kyphosis) is the classic deformity of spinal tuberculosis.

Osteomyelitis & Bone Infections 13 questions

Chronic osteomyelitis

Must-doRepeated6 past questions

Chronic osteomyelitis: dead bone (sequestrum) inside new bone (involucrum), with sinuses discharging through cloacae. Staph aureus is the commonest cause (Salmonella in sickle cell). Treatment: surgical removal of the sequestrum plus long antibiotics. Long-standing sinuses can turn into squamous cell cancer (Marjolin ulcer).
