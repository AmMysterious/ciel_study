Airway Management & Pre-Anaesthetic Evaluation 56 questions

Airway Management & Pre-Anaesthetic Evaluation (overview)

Must-doRepeated22 past questions

Pre-anaesthetic evaluation assesses cardiopulmonary reserve, airway difficulty, fasting status, and perioperative risks before surgery. Standard fasting guidelines follow the 2-4-6-8 rule, mandating minimum fasting times of 2 hours for clear liquids, 4 hours for breast milk, 6 hours for light meals, and 8 hours for fatty meals. Airway assessment incorporates history, inter-incisor distance (normal > 3 cm), thyromental distance (normal > 6.5 cm), and neck mobility. Capnography and pulse oximetry are mandatory monitoring standards throughout general anaesthesia and airway instrumentation.

Mallampati classification

Must-doRepeated10 past questions

The modified Mallampati classification evaluates oropharyngeal structures visible with the patient sitting upright, mouth open wide, and tongue fully protruded without phonation. Class I visualises the soft palate, fauces, uvula, and pillars; Class II visualises the soft palate, fauces, and uvula; Class III visualises the soft palate and base of the uvula; Class IV visualises the hard palate only. Classes III and IV correlate strongly with Cormack-Lehane laryngoscopy grades 3 and 4, predicting difficult direct laryngoscopy and tracheal intubation.

Difficult airway

Must-doRepeated11 past questions

Difficult airway encompasses difficulty with face mask ventilation, supraglottic airway placement, laryngoscopy, or tracheal intubation. Cormack-Lehane laryngoscopy grading ranges from Grade 1 with a full glottic view to Grade 4 where neither glottis nor epiglottis is visible. Awake flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy is the gold-standard investigation and technique of choice for anticipated difficult airway. In a cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate crisis, emergency front-of-neck access via scalpel-bougie-tube cricothyroidotomy is the definitive life-saving intervention.

Endotracheal intubation basics

Must-doRepeated15 past questions

Endotracheal tubes maintain airway patency and protect against pulmonary aspiration during general anaesthesia and mechanical ventilation. Tube size denotes internal diameter in millimetres, typically 7.0 to 7.5 mm for women and 8.0 to 8.5 mm for men, positioned at a depth of approximately 21 cm in women and 23 cm in men at the corner of the mouth. In paediatric patients older than 1 year, uncuffed tube size is estimated as (Age/4) + 4 mm, and cuffed tube size as (Age/4) + 3.5 mm. Continuous waveform capnography showing sustained end-tidal carbon dioxide across at least five consecutive breaths is the gold standard for confirming correct tracheal placement.

ASA classification

Must-doRepeated5 past questions

The American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification stratifies preoperative systemic health and perioperative mortality. ASA I is a normal healthy patient; ASA II has mild systemic disease without functional limitations; ASA III has severe systemic disease with substantive functional limitations; ASA IV has severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life. ASA V represents a moribund patient not expected to survive 24 hours without surgery, ASA VI is a declared brain-dead organ donor, and an 'E' suffix denotes emergency surgery.

Perioperative drug management

Must-doRepeated9 past questions

Perioperative medication management balances surgical bleeding risks against disease decompensation. Essential cardiovascular drugs like beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and statins are continued on the morning of surgery with a sip of water, while ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are withheld 24 hours prior to prevent refractory hypotension. Oral hypoglycaemics are withheld on the morning of surgery, and SGLT2 inhibitors are stopped 3 days before (risk of euglycaemic ketoacidosis); sugars are covered with insulin. Antiplatelets and anticoagulants are discontinued beforehand based on procedure risk, holding clopidogrel for 5 to 7 days and warfarin for 5 days to reach an INR under 1.5.

General Anaesthesia Drugs 40 questions

General Anaesthesia Drugs (overview)

Must-doRepeated16 past questions

General anaesthesia produces a reversible state of unconsciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and immobility with attenuation of autonomic stress responses. Balanced anaesthesia combines an intravenous induction agent for hypnosis, an opioid for analgesia, and a neuromuscular blocker for muscle relaxation. Depth of anaesthesia is monitored clinically and through processed electroencephalography like the Bispectral Index, targeting a range of 40 to 60 for surgical planes. Smooth emergence requires reversal of residual neuromuscular blockade and titration of postoperative analgesia.

IV agents: Propofol, Ketamine, Etomidate, Thiopentone

Must-doRepeated19 past questions

Propofol is the intravenous induction agent of choice for routine and day-care surgery due to rapid onset, clear-headed recovery, and intrinsic antiemetic actions, though it causes dose-dependent hypotension and respiratory depression. Ketamine acts as an NMDA receptor antagonist producing dissociative anaesthesia with preserved airway reflexes, bronchodilation, and sympathetic stimulation, making it the induction agent of choice in shock, asthma, and hypovolaemia, though it can cause emergence delirium. Etomidate provides outstanding cardiovascular stability with minimal change in blood pressure, making it the induction drug of choice in severe cardiac disease, but it transiently inhibits adrenal steroidogenesis via 11-beta-hydroxylase. Thiopentone is an ultrashort-acting barbiturate with potent anticonvulsant and neuroprotective properties that reduces intracranial pressure, but is strictly contraindicated in acute intermittent porphyria.

Inhalational agents

Must-doRepeated17 past questions

Inhalational anaesthetic potency is measured by Minimum Alveolar Concentration (MAC), where lower MAC reflects higher potency. Desflurane has the lowest blood-gas partition coefficient (0.42), conferring the fastest induction and emergence, but its airway pungency precludes inhalational induction. Sevoflurane (blood-gas coefficient 0.69) is pleasant-smelling and non-irritant, making it the inhalational agent of choice for paediatric and smooth mask inductions, though it degrades to Compound A in carbon dioxide absorbents. Halothane causes halothane hepatitis and arrhythmias, while nitrous oxide has a MAC of 104%, exhibits the second-gas effect, and expands closed gas spaces like pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, and tympanoplasty.

Local Anaesthetics & Toxicity (LAST) 28 questions

Local Anaesthetics & Toxicity (LAST) (overview)

Must-doRepeated13 past questions

Local anaesthetics reversibly block voltage-gated sodium channels from the intracellular side of the axonal membrane, halting nerve impulse propagation. They are classified into amino esters metabolised by plasma pseudocholinesterase with higher allergy risk via para-aminobenzoic acid, and amino amides metabolised by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes. The uncharged lipid-soluble form penetrates the neuronal sheath, whereas the charged cation binds the receptor inside the sodium channel pore. Tissue acidosis in infected areas reduces the uncharged fraction, markedly delaying onset and diminishing anaesthetic efficacy.

Drugs: Lidocaine, Bupivacaine

Must-doRepeated12 past questions

Lidocaine is an intermediate-acting amino amide with rapid onset, having a maximum safe dose of about 3-4.5 mg/kg plain and 7 mg/kg with adrenaline (1:200,000). Adrenaline produces local vasoconstriction that prolongs anaesthetic duration and reduces systemic absorption, but is contraindicated in end-arterial territories like digits, penis, and the tip of the nose. Bupivacaine is a long-acting amino amide with high lipid solubility and marked sensory block, widely used for spinal, epidural, and regional blocks with a maximum safe dose of 2 mg/kg plain and 2.5 mg/kg with adrenaline. Bupivacaine dissociates very slowly from cardiac sodium channels, conferring high cardiotoxicity; its pure S-enantiomer levobupivacaine and ropivacaine have significantly safer cardiac profiles.

Toxicity and 20% intralipid

Must-doRepeated8 past questions

Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity results from accidental intravascular injection or massive systemic absorption, classically seen with bupivacaine. Central nervous system toxicity manifests initially with perioral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, and lightheadedness, rapidly progressing to tonic-clonic convulsions and coma. Cardiovascular toxicity follows with refractory ventricular arrhythmias, conduction block, and severe myocardial depression. Immediate management prioritises airway control with 100% oxygen, seizure suppression with benzodiazepines, and prompt intravenous administration of 20% lipid emulsion as an initial 1.5 mL/kg bolus followed by continuous infusion.

Ventilation & Oxygenation 28 questions

Ventilation & Oxygenation (overview)

High-yield7 past questions

Intraoperative and ICU ventilatory management maintains arterial oxygenation and carbon dioxide elimination while mitigating ventilator-induced lung injury. Positive pressure ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, reducing venous return, right ventricular preload, and cardiac output, especially in hypovolaemic patients. The alveolar gas equation calculates alveolar partial pressure of oxygen (PAO2), enabling evaluation of the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient to distinguish hypoventilation from intrinsic lung disease. Lung-protective ventilation utilizing low tidal volumes (6 to 8 mL/kg of predicted body weight) and positive end-expiratory pressure prevents barotrauma and volutrauma.

Oxygen delivery devices & flow rates

Must-do11 past questions

Oxygen therapy devices are categorized into low-flow and high-flow systems depending on whether device flow meets total inspiratory demand. Nasal cannulas deliver 24% to 44% FiO2 at flow rates of 1 to 6 L/min, with each L/min increasing FiO2 by approximately 4%. Simple face masks deliver 35% to 55% FiO2 at 5 to 8 L/min, requiring at least 5 L/min flow to prevent carbon dioxide rebreathing. Non-rebreather masks with reservoir bags deliver 60% to 90% FiO2 at 10 to 15 L/min. Venturi masks utilize the Bernoulli principle to provide precise, guaranteed FiO2 (24% to 60%), making them the oxygen delivery device of choice in patients with COPD and chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure.

Ventilator basics

High-yield9 past questions

Mechanical ventilation modes are categorized by triggering, cycling, and control variables into volume-controlled or pressure-controlled ventilation. Volume-controlled ventilation delivers a preset tidal volume with variable airway pressure, while pressure-controlled ventilation delivers a preset inspiratory pressure with variable tidal volume. Peak inspiratory pressure reflects airway resistance and lung compliance, while plateau pressure measured during an end-inspiratory pause reflects static alveolar compliance and must remain below 30 cm H2O to prevent alveolar rupture. Positive end-expiratory pressure keeps alveoli open at end-expiration, increasing functional residual capacity and improving oxygenation in acute respiratory distress syndrome.

Hypoxia management

High-yield5 past questions

Hypoxia in an anaesthetised or mechanically ventilated patient is an acute crisis requiring immediate 100% oxygen delivery and systematic evaluation using the DOPE mnemonic (Dislodgement, Obstruction, Pneumothorax, Equipment failure). Causes of intraoperative hypoxemia include low inspired oxygen fraction, hypoventilation, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, right-to-left shunt, and diffusion impairment. Pulse oximetry non-invasively monitors arterial oxygen saturation, but yields false readings with carboxyhemoglobin, methemoglobin, severe vasoconstriction, and nail polish. Arterial blood gas analysis is the gold-standard investigation of choice to assess PaO2, PaCO2, and acid-base status during persistent hypoxia.

High-flow nasal cannula

High-yield3 past questions

High-flow nasal cannula delivers heated, humidified oxygen-air mixtures at flow rates up to 60 L/min with adjustable FiO2 from 21% to 100%. The high flow generates low-level positive end-expiratory pressure (approximately 1 cm H2O per 10 L/min flow with mouth closed), washes out upper airway anatomical dead space, and reduces work of breathing. Matching or exceeding patient peak inspiratory flow ensures constant, reliable FiO2 delivery. The ROX index (SpO2/FiO2 divided by respiratory rate) assesses treatment response; a ROX index of 4.88 or greater after 12 hours predicts successful therapy and low risk of intubation.

Anaesthesia Complications 25 questions

Anaesthesia Complications (overview)

Must-do13 past questions

Anaesthetic complications range from common minor postoperative symptoms like sore throat and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) to life-threatening intraoperative crises. Risk factors for PONV include female gender, non-smoking status, history of motion sickness, and use of postoperative opioids, managed with combination prophylaxis like ondansetron and dexamethasone. Unintended awareness under general anaesthesia occurs most frequently in high-risk settings like cardiac surgery, trauma, and obstetric emergencies. Unintentional hypothermia develops from anaesthetic-induced vasodilation and cold operating rooms, leading to coagulopathy, delayed drug metabolism, and wound infection, prevented with active forced-air warmers.

Malignant hyperthermia

Must-do11 past questions

Malignant hyperthermia is a rare pharmacogenetic disorder of skeletal muscle calcium regulation inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with mutations in the RYR1 or CACNA1S genes. It is triggered by volatile inhalational anaesthetics (halothane, isoflurane, sevoflurane, desflurane) and depolarising muscle relaxants (succinylcholine). The earliest and most reliable sign is an abrupt, unexplained rise in end-tidal CO2 refractory to hyperventilation, accompanied by tachycardia, masseter spasm, generalised muscle rigidity, and rapidly rising core temperature. Immediate management requires discontinuing triggering agents, hyperventilating with 100% oxygen at high flows, active cooling, and prompt intravenous administration of dantrolene sodium (2.5 mg/kg bolus repeated as needed), which blocks ryanodine receptor calcium release.

Air embolism

Must-do5 past questions

Venous air embolism occurs when subatmospheric venous pressure allows ambient air into the circulation, classically seen during neurosurgery in the sitting position, central line placement, and laparoscopy. Transoesophageal echocardiography is the most sensitive way to detect air in the heart, and precordial Doppler is the most sensitive non-invasive monitor. Clinical signs include a sudden decrease in end-tidal CO2, acute hypotension, tachycardia, hypoxia, and a classic mill-wheel murmur over the precordium. Immediate management requires flooding the surgical field with saline, positioning the patient in the Durant position (left lateral decubitus with slight Trendelenburg), administering 100% oxygen, and aspirating air through a central venous catheter.

Muscle Relaxants & Neuromuscular Block 22 questions

Muscle Relaxants & Neuromuscular Block (overview)

High-yieldRepeatedINI-CET7 past questions

Neuromuscular blocking agents act at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on the motor end-plate to produce skeletal muscle paralysis without providing sedation or analgesia. Depolarising blockers act as persistent agonists that cause prolonged end-plate depolarization, manifested initially by transient muscle fasciculations. Non-depolarising blockers competitively antagonise nicotinic receptors without activating them. Neuromuscular monitoring utilizes peripheral nerve stimulation with train-of-four; a train-of-four ratio of 0.9 or greater at the adductor pollicis is mandatory before safe extubation to exclude residual paralysis.

Depolarizing vs non-depolarizing

High-yieldRepeatedINI-CET3 past questions

Depolarising neuromuscular block (Phase I block) produced by succinylcholine is characterized by absence of post-tetanic potentiation, absence of train-of-four fade, and augmentation of block by anticholinesterase agents. High or repeated doses of succinylcholine produce a Phase II (dual) block that mimics non-depolarising block with train-of-four fade and tetanic fade. Non-depolarising block exhibits prominent train-of-four fade, post-tetanic potentiation, and is reversed by acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. Atracurium and cisatracurium undergo organ-independent elimination via Hofmann elimination and ester hydrolysis, making them the muscle relaxants of choice in renal failure and hepatic failure.

Suxamethonium complications

Must-doRepeatedINI-CET7 past questions

Succinylcholine has the fastest onset (30 to 60 seconds) and shortest duration of all muscle relaxants, making it the drug of choice for rapid sequence intubation, but carries substantial adverse effects. Transient generalised muscle fasciculations cause postoperative myalgia and transiently elevate intraocular, intragastric, and intracranial pressures. It triggers acute hyperkalemia by releasing intracellular potassium, causing dangerous arrhythmias in burns, crush injuries, denervation, and muscular dystrophies. It is a potent trigger for malignant hyperthermia, can cause bradycardia with repeated doses, and causes prolonged apnea in patients with atypical plasma pseudocholinesterase.

Reversal agents

High-yieldRepeatedINI-CET10 past questions

Reversal of competitive non-depolarising neuromuscular block is conventionally achieved with anticholinesterase agents like neostigmine, which increase acetylcholine concentration at the motor end-plate. Neostigmine must always be co-administered with an antimuscarinic agent like glycopyrrolate to prevent parasympathetic side effects such as severe bradycardia, bronchospasm, and salivation. Sugammadex is a modified gamma-cyclodextrin that selectively encapsulates and inactivates aminosteroid relaxants (rocuronium > vecuronium), achieving rapid reversal without anticholinergic side effects. Sugammadex reverses deep rocuronium blockade within minutes, providing a critical rescue option in cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate scenarios following rocuronium induction.

Monitoring in Anaesthesia 20 questions

Capnography (EtCO2)

Must-doRepeated16 past questions

Capnography provides continuous graphical measurement of carbon dioxide concentration in respiratory gases, where end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) normally measures 35 to 45 mm Hg. A normal capnogram exhibits Phase I (inspiratory baseline), Phase II (expiratory upstroke), Phase III (alveolar plateau), and Phase 0 (inspiratory downstroke). A shark-fin appearance with a prolonged expiratory upslope is pathognomonic of bronchospasm or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Sudden loss of EtCO2 indicates endotracheal tube disconnection, esophageal intubation, or complete airway obstruction, while a sudden drop in EtCO2 with preserved ventilation points to pulmonary embolism, air embolism, or cardiac arrest.

Brain Death & ICU Concepts 15 questions

Brain Death & ICU Concepts (overview)

High-yieldINI-CET3 past questions

Critical care in the ICU integrates organ support, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, and end-of-life determinations for critically ill patients. Shock is categorized into septic, hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, and obstructive forms, with noradrenaline serving as the first-line vasopressor of choice in septic shock. Arterial lines provide continuous beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring, while central venous catheters allow vasoactive infusions and central venous oxygen saturation monitoring. Brain death is defined as the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, confirmed by absence of brainstem reflexes and a positive apnea test.

Glasgow Coma Scale

High-yieldINI-CET10 past questions

The Glasgow Coma Scale provides a standardised assessment of level of consciousness based on three objective behavioral responses: Eye opening (1 to 4), Verbal response (1 to 5), and Motor response (1 to 6), yielding a composite score from 3 to 15. Eye opening ranges from spontaneous (4) to sound (3), pressure (2), and none (1); Verbal response ranges from oriented (5) to confused (4), words (3), sounds (2), and none (1); Motor response ranges from obeys commands (6) to localising (5), normal flexion (4), abnormal flexion/decorticate (3), extension/decerebrate (2), and none (1). A GCS score of 8 or less defines severe head injury and coma, requiring immediate endotracheal intubation to secure and protect the airway.

Pain Management & Opioids 6 questions

Pain Management & Opioids

AskedRepeated6 past questions

Acute and chronic pain management follows the WHO analgesic ladder, combining non-opioids (paracetamol, NSAIDs), weak opioids (tramadol, codeine), and strong opioids (morphine, fentanyl) alongside adjuvant therapies. Paracetamol provides analgesia and antipyresis via central cyclooxygenase inhibition; overdose causes severe hepatotoxicity due to toxic NAPQI accumulation, treated with N-acetylcysteine. Opioids act on mu-opioid receptors to inhibit presynaptic neurotransmitter release, causing analgesia alongside respiratory depression, sedation, constipation, nausea, and miosis (tolerance does not develop to constipation and miosis). Morphine has active polar metabolites that accumulate in renal impairment, whereas fentanyl is metabolized hepatically without active renal metabolites, making it the opioid of choice in renal failure.
