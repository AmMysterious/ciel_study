Hematopathology 189 questions

Anemias

Must-do92 past questions

Haemolysis: low haptoglobin, high LDH, unconjugated bilirubin, reticulocytosis. Sickle cell: HbS (valine for glutamic acid at position 6), crises with bone pain. Hereditary spherocytosis: osmotic fragility, high MCHC, cured by splenectomy. G6PD: Heinz bodies, bite cells after oxidant drugs. Thalassaemia: target cells, HbA2 raised in trait.

Try a past question

A 12-year-old child has recurrent episodes of bone pain, anemia and jaundice. What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Thalassemia major
  2. B. Hereditary spherocytosis
  3. C. Sickle cell anemia
  4. D. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia

Answer: C. Recurrent bone pain crises with anaemia and jaundice in a child point to sickle cell anaemia.

Leukemias

Lymphomas

Must-doRepeated34 past questions

Hodgkin: Reed-Sternberg cells (CD15, CD30); nodular sclerosis is the commonest type, lymphocyte-rich has the best outlook. Non-Hodgkin: Burkitt t(8;14) c-MYC, starry sky; follicular t(14;18) BCL-2; mantle cell t(11;14) cyclin D1. An excision biopsy of the whole node is the diagnostic test.

Neoplasia 80 questions

Oncogenes

Must-doRepeated14 past questions

Oncogenes need one hit (gain of function): RAS (commonest), MYC, HER2/neu, BCR-ABL, RET, KIT. Tumour suppressors need two hits (loss of function). Point mutation (RAS), amplification (MYC, HER2) and translocation (BCR-ABL) switch oncogenes on.

  1. A. Tumor suppressor gene
  2. B. DNA repair gene
  3. C. Apoptotic gene
  4. D. Oncogene

Answer: D. A growth signal that stays on without its ligand comes from an activated oncogene.

Tumor suppressor genes

Must-doRepeated25 past questions

p53 (guardian of the genome): stops the cell cycle at G1/S to allow repair, or triggers apoptosis; the commonest mutated gene in cancer; Li-Fraumeni. RB: retinoblastoma and osteosarcoma (two-hit hypothesis). APC: familial polyposis. BRCA1/2: breast and ovary. NF1, WT1, VHL.

Try a past question

  1. A. Angiogenesis
  2. B. Growth factor secretion
  3. C. Cell cycle arrest
  4. D. Metastasis

Answer: C. p53 halts the cell cycle after DNA damage so it can be repaired (or the cell dies).

Molecular pathology: microsatellite instability, Lynch syndrome

Must-doRepeated3 past questions

Lynch syndrome (HNPCC): mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2), microsatellite instability, right-sided colon cancers, endometrial cancer. Familial polyposis: APC, hundreds of polyps. BRCA defects: homologous recombination repair.

  1. A. APC mutation
  2. B. DNA mismatch repair gene mutation

  4. D. BCL-2 overexpression

Answer: B. Lynch syndrome comes from defective DNA mismatch repair, which shows as microsatellite instability.

Metastasis

Must-do6 past questions

Carcinomas spread by lymph; sarcomas by blood (exceptions: renal cell, liver and follicular thyroid cancers spread by blood). Bone metastases: breast, prostate (osteoblastic), lung, kidney, thyroid. The commonest malignant liver tumour is a metastasis.

Cell Injury & Cell Death 54 questions

Reversible and irreversible injury

Must-doRepeated12 past questions

Reversible injury: cell swelling (the earliest change), fatty change, blebs. Irreversible: mitochondrial damage, membrane rupture, nuclear changes (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis). Adaptations: hypertrophy (bigger cells), hyperplasia (more cells), atrophy, metaplasia (one adult cell type replaced by another).

Try a past question

A cell contains abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and elongated, centrally located nuclei with prominent nucleoli. Which cellular adaptation is characterized by an increase in cell size?

  1. A. Hyperplasia
  2. B. Hypertrophy
  3. C. Metaplasia
  4. D. Dysplasia

Answer: B. An increase in cell size is hypertrophy; an increase in cell number is hyperplasia.

Necrosis

Must-doRepeated16 past questions

Coagulative: most infarcts (heart, kidney); outlines kept. Liquefactive: brain infarcts and abscesses. Caseous: TB. Fat necrosis: pancreatitis and breast (chalky deposits). Fibrinoid: vasculitis and malignant hypertension. Gangrene: wet (infected) or dry.

Try a past question

  1. A. Liquefactive necrosis
  2. B. Caseous necrosis
  3. C. Coagulative necrosis
  4. D. Fat necrosis

Answer: C. Preserved cell outlines with fading nuclei in an infarct are coagulative necrosis.

Apoptosis

Must-doRepeated14 past questions

Programmed cell death without inflammation: shrinkage, condensed chromatin, apoptotic bodies. Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway: cytochrome c release, BCL-2 family control it; extrinsic: Fas and TNF receptors. Caspases carry it out. Councilman bodies in hepatitis are apoptotic cells.

Try a past question

  1. A. Extrinsic pathway
  2. B. Intrinsic pathway
  3. C. Necrosis
  4. D. Autophagy

Answer: B. Cytochrome c release from mitochondria activates the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis.

Inflammation & Repair 53 questions

Acute inflammation and chemical mediators

Must-doRepeated23 past questions

Histamine (from mast cells) causes the first vasodilation and leakiness. Neutrophils arrive first (6-24 hours), then macrophages. Steps: margination, rolling (selectins), adhesion (integrins), transmigration (PECAM-1), chemotaxis (C5a, IL-8, LTB4). Fever: IL-1, IL-6, TNF. Albumin falls (negative acute phase protein); CRP and ferritin rise.

Try a past question

  1. A. Histamine
  2. B. Interferon-gamma
  3. C. Transforming growth factor-beta
  4. D. Interleukin-2

Answer: A. Histamine is the main early mediator of vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.

Chronic and granulomatous inflammation

Must-doRepeated20 past questions

Granuloma: a collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with giant cells (Langhans in TB, foreign body type). Formed by type IV (T-cell) hypersensitivity with IFN-γ. Caseating: TB; non-caseating: sarcoidosis, Crohn disease.

Try a past question

  1. A. Type I hypersensitivity
  2. B. Type II hypersensitivity
  3. C. Type III hypersensitivity
  4. D. Type IV hypersensitivity

Answer: D. TB granulomas are a type IV, T-cell mediated reaction.

Wound healing

Must-doRepeated10 past questions

Order: inflammation, then proliferation (granulation tissue, angiogenesis by VEGF), then remodelling. Type III collagen first, replaced by type I. Scar strength reaches about 70-80% of normal at 3 months. Vitamin C and zinc are needed; keloids extend beyond the wound edge.

Try a past question

  3. C. Inflammation →Proliferation → Remodeling
  4. D. Remodeling→Inflammation→Proliferation

Answer: C. Healing goes inflammation, then proliferation, then remodelling.

Immunopathology 83 questions

Hypersensitivity

High-yieldRepeated28 past questions

Type I: IgE and mast cells (anaphylaxis, asthma, hay fever). Type II: antibodies against cells (transfusion reactions, Goodpasture, myasthenia, Graves). Type III: immune complexes (SLE, serum sickness, post-strep GN, Arthus). Type IV: T cells (TB granuloma, Mantoux, contact dermatitis, graft rejection).

Autoimmune diseases

High-yieldRepeated28 past questions

Goodpasture: anti-GBM antibodies against type IV collagen, lung bleeding and nephritis (linear IgG on immunofluorescence). SLE: anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith. Sjögren: anti-Ro and anti-La. Most autoimmune diseases are commoner in women.

Transplant rejection

High-yield17 past questions

Hyperacute rejection (minutes): pre-formed antibodies, type II. Acute (weeks to months): T cells and antibodies; treatable. Chronic (months to years): vascular narrowing and fibrosis. Graft-versus-host disease: donor T cells attack skin, gut and liver, after bone marrow transplants.

Renal Pathology 54 questions

Nephrotic syndromes

High-yieldRepeated22 past questions

Minimal change: children, podocyte foot process effacement on electron microscopy, steroid-responsive. FSGS: commonest in adults in many places, HIV and heroin. Membranous: adults, spike and dome on silver stain, PLA2R antibodies. Diabetic: Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules.

Nephritic syndromes

High-yieldRepeated27 past questions

Haematuria, red cell casts, hypertension, oedema. Post-strep: subepithelial humps, low C3. IgA nephropathy: haematuria 1-2 days after a sore throat, the commonest GN worldwide. Rapidly progressive (crescents): Goodpasture, ANCA vasculitis, immune complex. Alport: collagen IV, deafness, eye changes.

Hemostasis & Thrombosis 53 questions

Coagulation cascade

High-yieldRepeated15 past questions

PT tests the extrinsic pathway (factor VII) and warfarin; aPTT tests the intrinsic pathway (VIII, IX, XI, XII) and heparin. Vitamin K deficiency prolongs PT first (factor VII has the shortest half-life); haemophilia prolongs only aPTT. Bleeding time tests platelets.

Try a past question

Which laboratory finding best differentiates between vitamin K deficiency and haemophilia A?

  1. A. aPTT (activated partial thromboplastin time)
  2. B. PT (prothrombin time)
  3. C. Bleeding time
  4. D. Platelet count

Answer: B. Vitamin K deficiency prolongs PT; haemophilia A leaves PT normal.

Hemophilia, vWD, DIC, TTP, HUS

High-yieldRepeated30 past questions

von Willebrand disease: the commonest inherited bleeding disorder, long bleeding time with long aPTT. DIC: low platelets and fibrinogen, high D-dimer, schistocytes. TTP: ADAMTS13 deficiency (pentad: fever, low platelets, haemolysis, kidney, brain). ITP is the commonest reason for elective splenectomy.

Thrombosis

High-yieldRepeated12 past questions

Virchow triad: endothelial injury, stasis, hypercoagulability. Factor V Leiden is the commonest inherited cause of a clotting tendency. Lines of Zahn show a clot formed in flowing blood (before death). Leg vein clots travel to the pulmonary arteries.

Answer: B. A clot from the leg veins travels through the right heart and lodges in the pulmonary artery.

Cardiovascular Pathology 51 questions

Myocardial infarction

High-yieldRepeated14 past questions

Changes over time: coagulative necrosis by 4-12 hours, neutrophils 1-3 days, macrophages 3-7 days (rupture risk highest at 3-7 days), granulation tissue 1-2 weeks, scar by 2 months. TTC stain shows the infarct pale against red healthy muscle in the first hours.

  3. C. congo red
  4. D. oil red O

Answer: A. Triphenyl tetrazolium chloride stains living muscle red and leaves the infarct pale.

Infective endocarditis

High-yieldRepeated15 past questions

Acute: Staph aureus on normal valves (IV drug users: tricuspid). Subacute: viridans strep on damaged valves. Signs: Osler nodes (painful), Janeway lesions (painless), Roth spots, splinter haemorrhages. Diagnosis: Duke criteria, blood cultures and echo.

Rheumatic fever

High-yield12 past questions

Follows group A strep throat infection by 2-3 weeks (molecular mimicry). Aschoff bodies with Anitschkow (caterpillar) cells. Jones criteria: carditis, arthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules. Mitral valve is most affected.

  3. C. Mallory–Denk body
  4. D. Negri body

Answer: A. Aschoff bodies contain Anitschkow cells with caterpillar-shaped nuclei; they are the mark of rheumatic carditis.

COPD

High-yield8 past questions

Hepatitis

High-yield3 past questions

Viral hepatitis: ballooning degeneration, Councilman (acidophil) bodies, ground-glass cells in hepatitis B. Alcoholic hepatitis: Mallory-Denk bodies, neutrophils, AST:ALT over 2. Autoimmune hepatitis: plasma cells, interface hepatitis.

Cirrhosis

High-yield4 past questions

Diffuse fibrosis with regenerating nodules. Causes in India: alcohol, hepatitis B and C, fatty liver. Leads to portal hypertension, liver failure and hepatocellular cancer. Wilson (copper) and haemochromatosis (iron, Prussian blue) are inherited causes.

Risk: hepatitis B and C, cirrhosis, aflatoxin (p53 mutation). AFP rises and is used to monitor treatment. Spreads through the blood (portal vein invasion). Fibrolamellar variant: young adults, normal AFP, better outlook.
