Neuro-Ophthalmology 62 questions

Visual pathway and field defects

Must-doRepeated19 past questions

Optic nerve lesion: blindness of that eye. Chiasm (pituitary tumour): bitemporal hemianopia. Optic tract or radiation: homonymous hemianopia on the opposite side. Temporal lobe (Meyer loop): upper quadrant loss ('pie in the sky'). Occipital lesion: homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.

Try a past question

A patient presents with left homonymous hemianopia due to a lesion in the visual pathway . Which of the following patterns of visual field loss is seen in this condition?

  1. A. Right temporal and Left nasal vision loss
  2. B. Right nasal and Left temporal vision loss
  3. C. Bilateral temporal vision loss
  4. D. Bilateral nasal vision loss

Answer: B. Left homonymous hemianopia means loss of the left half of the field: left temporal and right nasal fields.

Papilledema

Must-do5 past questions

Swollen discs on both sides from raised intracranial pressure; vision is kept until late (enlarged blind spot). Signs: blurred disc margins, loss of venous pulsation, haemorrhages. Find the cause (tumour, idiopathic intracranial hypertension).

Optic neuritis

Must-do15 past questions

Young woman, painful eye movements, sudden loss of vision, poor colour vision, relative afferent pupillary defect; the disc often looks normal (retrobulbar). Linked to multiple sclerosis. IV methylprednisolone speeds recovery (Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial).

Third nerve palsy

Must-do7 past questions

Third nerve palsy: the eye looks down and out, ptosis, a dilated pupil if the nerve is compressed (posterior communicating aneurysm). Diabetic (ischaemic) palsy usually spares the pupil. Fourth nerve: vertical diplopia worse looking down (stairs), head tilts away. Sixth: eye cannot turn out.

  1. A. 4th nerve palsy
  2. B. 3rd nerve palsy
  3. C. 6th nerve palsy
  4. D. 7th nerve palsy

Answer: B. Down-and-out eye with ptosis is a third nerve palsy.

Horner syndrome

High-yield7 past questions

Horner syndrome: ptosis (mild), miosis, anhidrosis (lack of sweating) on the same side, apparent enophthalmos. Causes: lung apex tumour (Pancoast), carotid dissection, brainstem stroke. Apraclonidine and cocaine drops help confirm it.

Try a past question

All of the following are features of Horner syndrome EXCEPT:

  1. A. Miosis
  2. B. Enophthalmos
  3. C. Hyperhidrosis
  4. D. Ptosis

Answer: C. Horner syndrome causes loss of sweating (anhidrosis), not excess sweating.

Glaucoma 51 questions

Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)

Must-doRepeated11 past questions

Open-angle glaucoma: painless, slow, peripheral field loss first; cupping of the optic disc (cup-disc ratio over 0.6, notching). Field defects: arcuate (Bjerrum) scotoma, nasal step. First-line drops: prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost); β-blockers (timolol).

Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)

Must-doRepeated18 past questions

Acute angle closure: sudden painful red eye, halos, headache and vomiting, a fixed mid-dilated oval pupil, very high pressure, shallow anterior chamber; older hypermetropic women. Lower the pressure (IV acetazolamide, pilocarpine once pressure falls), then laser peripheral iridotomy in both eyes.

Try a past question

  1. A. Angle closure glaucoma
  2. B. Optic neuritis
  3. C. CSR
  4. D. Cataract

Answer: A. Sudden painful loss of vision with a mid-dilated pupil is acute angle-closure glaucoma.

Secondary glaucoma

Must-do19 past questions

Glaucoma in one eye only is usually secondary: after uveitis, trauma (angle recession), lens problems (phacomorphic, phacolytic), steroids, or new vessels (neovascular glaucoma in diabetes and vein occlusion).

Try a past question

Unilateral (one-eyed) glaucoma is most commonly associated with which of the following?

  1. A. Secondary glaucoma
  2. B. Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG)
  3. C. Angle-Closure Glaucoma (PACG)
  4. D. Pseudophakia

Answer: A. A raised pressure in only one eye usually has a local cause: secondary glaucoma.

Retina 51 questions

Diabetic retinopathy

Must-doRepeated16 past questions

Non-proliferative: microaneurysms (earliest sign), dot-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton wool spots. Proliferative: new vessels on the disc or elsewhere; treat with pan-retinal photocoagulation (or anti-VEGF). Macular oedema is the commonest cause of vision loss; anti-VEGF injections.

  1. A. Focal laser to the macula
  2. B. Pan-retinal Photocoagulation (PRP)
  3. C. Cataract surgery
  4. D. Oral Vitamin A

Answer: B. New vessels on the disc mean proliferative retinopathy: pan-retinal photocoagulation.

Retinal detachment

Must-do15 past questions

Rhegmatogenous detachment (from a retinal break): flashes, floaters, then a curtain over the vision. Risk: high myopia, cataract surgery, trauma. Grey, billowing retina. Treat early with surgery (scleral buckle, vitrectomy, pneumatic retinopexy).

  1. A. Retinal detachment
  2. B. Macular degeneration
  3. C. Optic atrophy
  4. D. Acute anterior uveitis

Answer: A. A curtain falling over the vision is the classic symptom of retinal detachment.

Retinitis pigmentosa

Must-do12 past questions

Inherited loss of rods first: night blindness from youth, ring scotoma, tunnel vision. Fundus triad: bony spicule pigment, waxy pale disc, thin arterioles. ERG is reduced early. Linked syndromes: Usher (deafness), Laurence-Moon-Bardet-Biedl.

Try a past question

  1. A. Retinitis pigmentosa
  2. B. Age-related macular degeneration
  3. C. Central retinal artery occlusion
  4. D. Diabetic retinopathy

Answer: A. Night blindness getting worse from the teens points to retinitis pigmentosa.

Cataract 49 questions

Age-related cataract

Must-do8 past questions

Gradual painless blurring of vision, glare, poor night vision. Cortical (cuneiform) and nuclear cataracts are common; posterior subcapsular cataract with steroids and diabetes. Nuclear cataract can cause 'second sight' (better near vision for a while). Treat by surgery when it limits daily life.

Try a past question

A 68-year-old man presents with gradual painless diminution of vision and difficulty seeing at night. Examination shows a gray-white opacity in the lens. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Cataract
  2. B. Glaucoma
  3. C. Retinal detachment
  4. D. Optic neuritis

Answer: A. Gradual painless loss of vision with a grey-white lens opacity in an older man is cataract.

Congenital cataract

Must-doRepeated7 past questions

Named cataracts: Christmas tree (myotonic dystrophy), sunflower (Wilson disease, chalcosis), oil drop (galactosaemia), snowflake (diabetes), rosette (trauma), blue dot (common, harmless). Congenital rubella causes cataract. Remove a dense one early to prevent amblyopia.

Cataract surgery and its complications

Must-doRepeated30 past questions

Small incision or phaco surgery with an intraocular lens. Trypan blue stains the anterior capsule. After-cataract (posterior capsule opacification): Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy. Endophthalmitis is the dreaded early complication; cystoid macular oedema (Irvine-Gass) comes later.

  1. A. Trypan blue
  2. B. Indocyanine green
  3. C. Fluorescein
  4. D. Brilliant blue G

Answer: A. Trypan blue is used to stain and see the anterior lens capsule during surgery.

Cornea & Corneal Ulcers 41 questions

Bacterial keratitis

Must-do3 past questions

Bacterial ulcers: Pseudomonas (rapid, contact lenses), Staph, Pneumococcus (ulcus serpens with hypopyon). Scrape for Gram stain and culture first, then fortified antibiotic drops (cefazolin with tobramycin) or a fluoroquinolone, every hour.

Fungal keratitis

Must-doRepeated10 past questions

Follows injury with vegetable matter (farmers). Dry raised ulcer with feathery edges, satellite lesions, hypopyon that may not move. KOH mount shows hyphae. Treat with natamycin 5% (Fusarium, Aspergillus).

Viral keratitis

Must-doRepeated7 past questions

Herpes simplex: dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs (fluorescein), reduced corneal sensation. Treat with topical antivirals (aciclovir ointment, ganciclovir gel); never steroids on active epithelial disease. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus: Hutchinson sign (tip of the nose) predicts eye involvement.

  2. B. Topical acyclovir 3% with atropine
  3. C. Steroids
  4. D. Oral acyclovir alone

Answer: B. A dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs is herpes simplex keratitis, treated with topical aciclovir.

Acanthamoeba keratitis

Must-do9 past questions

Contact lens wearers, swimming or washing lenses in tap water. Pain far worse than the signs, a ring infiltrate, radial keratoneuritis. Cysts on calcofluor white or in culture on non-nutrient agar with E. coli. Treat with PHMB or chlorhexidine drops.

Lens & Refractive Errors 40 questions

Myopia

Must-doRepeated9 past questions

Short-sightedness: light focuses in front of the retina; corrected with concave (minus) lenses. School children squint to see the board. High myopia raises the risk of retinal detachment and glaucoma. Low-dose atropine slows progression in children.

Try a past question

A 9 year old boy is brought to the doctor with complaints of squeezing his eyes to see the blackboard in school. What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Myopia
  2. B. Hypermetropia
  3. C. Accommodative inertia
  4. D. Astigmatism

Answer: A. Squinting to see the blackboard (distance) is typical of myopia.

Hypermetropia

Must-doRepeated5 past questions

Long-sightedness: light focuses behind the retina; corrected with convex (plus) lenses. Children may get convergent squint (accommodative esotropia). A large difference between the eyes (anisometropia) causes amblyopia in the weaker eye. Hypermetropes are prone to angle-closure glaucoma.

Try a past question

What diagnosis is suggested by having different refractive powers +5D in one eye and +1D in the other eye?

  1. A. Refractive errors
  2. B. Amblyopia
  3. C. Anisometropic amblyopia
  4. D. Malingering

Answer: C. A big difference in power between the eyes (+5D vs +1D) causes anisometropic amblyopia.

Astigmatism

Must-doRepeated10 past questions

Unequal curvature of the cornea, so no single focus; corrected with cylindrical lenses. 'With the rule' astigmatism is common in the young. Keratoconus (cone-shaped thinning cornea): irregular astigmatism, Munson sign, Fleischer ring; treat with corneal cross-linking.

Ocular Trauma 38 questions

Chemical injury

Must-doRepeated7 past questions

Alkali burns are worse than acid (they go deeper, saponifying tissue). The first step is immediate, copious irrigation with water or saline for at least 30 minutes, until the pH is normal. Limbal ischaemia predicts the outcome.

Try a past question

  1. A. Instillation of topical antibiotics
  2. B. Topical corticosteroids
  3. C. Cycloplegic drops
  4. D. Copious irrigation with normal saline or water

Answer: D. Immediate copious irrigation is the single most important step in a chemical burn.

Blunt trauma

High-yield19 past questions

Hyphaema (blood in the anterior chamber), angle recession (later glaucoma), Vossius ring on the lens, commotio retinae (Berlin oedema), blow-out fracture of the orbital floor (diplopia on looking up, numb cheek, teardrop sign).

Try a past question

A patient presents with a blunt trauma injury to the eye from a tennis ball. Examination shows a collection of blood in the anterior chamber. What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Vitreous hemorrhage
  2. B. Hyphaema
  3. C. Hypopyon
  4. D. Traumatic cataract

Answer: B. Blood collected in the anterior chamber after blunt injury is a hyphaema.

Penetrating injury

High-yield4 past questions

Signs: a peaked pupil, shallow chamber, prolapsed uveal tissue, low pressure. Shield the eye, no pressure, no drops; X-ray or CT for a foreign body (no MRI if metal). Siderosis from retained iron, chalcosis from copper.

Sympathetic ophthalmia

High-yield5 past questions

Sympathetic ophthalmia: a bilateral granulomatous uveitis weeks to months after a penetrating injury to one eye (uveal antigens exposed). Dalen-Fuchs nodules. Prevented by removing a badly injured eye within about 2 weeks; treated with steroids.

Uveitis 36 questions

Anterior uveitis

Must-do24 past questions

Pain, redness around the cornea (ciliary flush), photophobia, small irregular pupil, keratic precipitates, cells and flare, hypopyon. Linked to HLA-B27 (ankylosing spondylitis). Treat with steroid drops and a cycloplegic (atropine) to break and prevent posterior synechiae (festooned pupil).

Try a past question

A patient presents with festooned pupils (an irregularly shaped pupil). Which drug is specifically used to break the posterior synechiae causing this appearance?

  1. A. Cyclopentolate
  2. B. Phenylephrine
  3. C. Atropine
  4. D. Scopolamine

Answer: C. Atropine dilates the pupil and breaks the posterior synechiae that cause a festooned pupil.

Posterior uveitis

Must-do4 past questions

Floaters and blurred vision with a quiet eye. Toxoplasma: the commonest cause; a focal necrotising retinochoroiditis next to an old scar with dense vitritis ('headlight in the fog'). CMV retinitis in AIDS (pizza-pie fundus).

  1. A. Ocular toxoplasmosis
  2. B. Multifocal choroiditis
  3. C. Ocular sarcoidosis
  4. D. Multiple evanescent white dot syndrome

Answer: A. A focal necrotic retinal lesion with heavy vitritis is ocular toxoplasmosis.

Systemic associations

Must-doRepeated9 past questions

HLA-B27 disease: acute anterior uveitis. Behçet: shifting hypopyon, oral and genital ulcers. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis: silent chronic anterior uveitis (screen ANA-positive children). Sarcoidosis: mutton-fat keratic precipitates. Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada: vitiligo, poliosis, meningitis.

Strabismus (Squint) 23 questions

Cover test, Hirschberg test

Must-do4 past questions

Hirschberg test: corneal light reflex; each 1 mm shift is about 7° (15 prism dioptres). Cover test finds a manifest squint; alternate cover test finds a latent one (phoria). Treat amblyopia early by patching the good eye.

Practise 4 questions on this, free on Telegram →

Paralytic squint

High-yieldRepeated11 past questions

Paralytic squint: double vision worse in the direction of the weak muscle, the deviation varies with gaze, secondary deviation larger than primary. Sixth nerve (the commonest): horizontal diplopia worse looking to the side of the palsy. Fourth nerve: trouble going downstairs, head tilt to the opposite side.

Try a past question

  1. A. Cranial Nerve 3 palsy
  2. B. Cranial Nerve 4 palsy
  3. C. Cranial Nerve 6 palsy
  4. D. Cranial Nerve 7 palsy

Answer: B. Vertical double vision worse on looking down, with a head tilt, is a fourth nerve palsy.

Ocular Tumors 18 questions

Retinoblastoma

Must-doRepeated14 past questions

Retinoblastoma: the commonest eye cancer in children; RB1 gene on chromosome 13 (two-hit). Leucocoria (white pupil, cat's eye reflex) is the commonest sign, then squint. Calcification on CT; Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes. Survivors of the inherited type risk osteosarcoma.

Try a past question

A child after a surgery of a ocular malignancy with round blue cell tumor , what is the gene responsible for the tumor development ?

  1. A. Rb gene
  2. B. P53 gene
  3. C. p57 gene
  4. D. RET

Answer: A. Retinoblastoma is caused by mutations in the RB gene.

Community Ophthalmology 18 questions

Vitamin A deficiency and xerophthalmia

Must-doRepeatedINI-CET13 past questions

WHO xerophthalmia stages: XN night blindness (earliest), X1A conjunctival xerosis, X1B Bitot spots, X2 corneal xerosis, X3A/X3B corneal ulcer and keratomalacia, XS scars, XF fundus changes. Treat with vitamin A on day 1, day 2 and after 2 weeks.

  1. A. X1A
  2. B. X1B
  3. C. X2
  4. D. X3A

Answer: B. Bitot's spots are stage X1B in the WHO classification.

Ophthalmic Instruments 5 questions

Eye investigations and instruments

Must-doRepeatedINI-CET5 past questions

Applanation tonometry (Goldmann) is the gold standard for eye pressure. Gonioscopy views the angle. A-scan biometry measures eye length for the lens power. Potential acuity meter predicts vision after cataract surgery. OCT images the macula and nerve fibre layer.

Try a past question

Which of the following is the investigation to predict post-operative visual outcome after cataract surgery?

  1. A. Pachymetry
  2. B. Topography
  3. C. Lensometry
  4. D. Potential acuity meter

Answer: D. A potential acuity meter estimates how well the eye will see after the cataract is removed.

Eyelid Disorders 21 questions

Ptosis

High-yield11 past questions

Congenital ptosis: weak levator. Marcus Gunn jaw-winking: the eyelid lifts on chewing (misdirected fifth nerve). Acquired: aponeurotic (old age), myasthenia (fatigable), third nerve palsy, Horner (mild). Surgery depends on levator function.

  2. B. Third nerve misdirection syndrome
  3. C. Marcus Gunn Jaw winking syndrome
  4. D. Oculomotor palsy

Answer: C. A ptotic lid that lifts with jaw movement is Marcus Gunn jaw-winking syndrome.

Conjunctiva 20 questions

Trachoma

High-yieldRepeated6 past questions

Chlamydia trachomatis A-C, the leading infectious cause of blindness. Follicles, Herbert pits, Arlt line (scar on the upper tarsal conjunctiva), trichiasis, corneal scarring. WHO SAFE strategy: Surgery, Antibiotics (azithromycin), Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement.

Vernal and allergic conjunctivitis

High-yield6 past questions

Vernal keratoconjunctivitis: boys, warm weather, intense itching, ropy discharge, cobblestone papillae on the upper lid, Horner-Trantas dots, shield ulcer. Treat with mast cell stabilisers and antihistamines; short steroid courses for flares.

Conjunctival tumors

High-yield3 past questions

Pterygium: a triangular fold growing onto the cornea from the nasal side, linked to sunlight; remove with a conjunctival autograft to stop recurrence. Pinguecula does not reach the cornea. Ocular surface squamous neoplasia is linked to HIV and HPV.

Lacrimal Apparatus 10 questions

Nasolacrimal duct obstruction

High-yieldRepeated7 past questions

Congenital nasolacrimal duct block (valve of Hasner): watering and discharge in infants. Massage (Crigler) until about 1 year; probing if it persists. Adults: dacryocystitis; dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) for chronic blockage.
