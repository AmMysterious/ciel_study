Neurophysiology 112 questions

Neuron physiology: membrane potential, action potential, conduction

Must-doRepeated35 past questions

Resting membrane potential (about −70 mV) is set mainly by potassium leak. Depolarisation: sodium in; repolarisation: potassium out. Conduction is faster in thicker, myelinated fibres (saltatory, at nodes of Ranvier). Fastest: Aα fibres; slowest: C fibres (unmyelinated).

Try a past question

Which factor primarily determines the rate of nerve impulse conduction in myelinated fibers?

  1. A. Amplitude of action potential
  2. B. Resting membrane potential
  3. C. Neurotransmitter release
  4. D. Diameter of the nerve fiber

Answer: D. In myelinated fibres conduction speed rises with fibre diameter.

Synapse and neurotransmitters

Must-doRepeated12 past questions

Main excitatory transmitter in the brain: glutamate; main inhibitory: GABA (spinal cord also glycine). Neuromuscular junction: acetylcholine on nicotinic receptors. Synaptic delay about 0.5 ms; fatigue at synapses is from transmitter depletion.

Answer: C. GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter in the adult brain.

Sensory pathways

Must-doRepeated39 past questions

Dorsal column (medial lemniscus): fine touch, vibration, position; crosses in the medulla. Spinothalamic: pain and temperature; crosses in the cord near entry. Utricle and saccule sense linear acceleration and head position; semicircular canals sense rotation.

Motor pathways: UMN vs LMN

Must-doRepeated15 past questions

Upper motor neurone lesion: spasticity, brisk reflexes, upgoing plantar (Babinski), no wasting early. Lower motor neurone: flaccid, wasting, fasciculations, absent reflexes. The muscle spindle senses stretch (stretch reflex); the Golgi tendon organ senses tension (inverse stretch reflex).

  4. D. Ruffini ending

Answer: B. The muscle spindle senses stretch and drives the stretch (knee-jerk) reflex.

Basal ganglia

Must-do6 past questions

Basal ganglia plan and smooth movement. Direct pathway (D1) eases movement; indirect (D2) suppresses it. Loss of nigral dopamine = Parkinson (hypokinetic). Caudate damage = Huntington chorea; subthalamic nucleus lesion = hemiballismus on the other side.

Cerebellum

Must-do5 past questions

The cerebellum coordinates movement on the same side. Signs: intention tremor, dysmetria (past-pointing), dysdiadochokinesia, ataxic gait, nystagmus, scanning speech, low tone. Vermis lesions affect the trunk; hemisphere lesions the limbs.

  4. D. Right-sided loss of pain and temperature

Answer: B. A cerebellar hemisphere lesion causes intention tremor and dysmetria on the same side.

Renal Physiology 101 questions

Glomerular filtration

Must-doRepeated29 past questions

GFR is about 125 mL/min, best measured by inulin clearance (creatinine in practice). Constricting the afferent arteriole lowers GFR; constricting the efferent raises it (angiotensin II does this). Autoregulation: myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback (macula densa).

Renal clearance

Must-doRepeated12 past questions

Clearance = urine concentration × urine flow ÷ plasma concentration. Inulin clearance = GFR. PAH clearance = renal plasma flow (about 625 mL/min) and is the highest, since PAH is filtered and secreted. Glucose clearance is normally zero.

Try a past question

  1. A. Inulin
  2. B. Creatinine
  3. C. Urea
  4. D. Para-aminohippuric acid

Answer: D. PAH is filtered and secreted almost completely, so its clearance is the highest.

Tubular transport

Must-do33 past questions

The proximal tubule reabsorbs about 65% of sodium and water, all glucose and amino acids. The thick ascending limb is impermeable to water (Na-K-2Cl pump; loop diuretics act here). Distal tubule: thiazides; collecting duct: ADH (aquaporin-2) and aldosterone.

Try a past question

  1. A. Proximal tubule
  2. B. Descending loop of Henle
  3. C. Ascending loop of Henle
  4. D. Collecting duct

Answer: C. The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salt out but lets no water follow.

Concentration & dilution (countercurrent mechanism)

Must-doRepeated18 past questions

The loop of Henle is the countercurrent multiplier; the vasa recta are the countercurrent exchanger. Urea recycling adds to medullary concentration. Maximum urine concentration about 1,200-1,400 mOsm/kg; ADH decides how much water the collecting duct takes back.

Try a past question

  1. A. Proximal convoluted tubule
  2. B. Loop of Henle
  3. C. Collecting duct
  4. D. All the above

Answer: B. The loop of Henle builds the medullary concentration gradient: it is the countercurrent multiplier.

Acid-base physiology

Must-do19 past questions

Bicarbonate is the most important buffer outside cells; haemoglobin is the main buffer of CO2 in blood; phosphate and proteins work inside cells. The kidney adds new bicarbonate by excreting H+ as ammonium and titratable acid. Normal pH 7.35-7.45, bicarbonate 22-26, PaCO2 35-45.

Try a past question

Which buffer system is the most important extracellular buffer in blood?

  1. A. Phosphate buffer system
  2. B. Protein buffer system
  3. C. Bicarbonate buffer system
  4. D. Haemoglobin buffer system

Answer: C. The bicarbonate system is the main extracellular buffer because both its parts are regulated (lungs and kidneys).

Endocrine Physiology 90 questions

Pituitary hormones

Must-doRepeated22 past questions

Anterior pituitary: GH, prolactin, ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH. Posterior (made in the hypothalamus): ADH and oxytocin. Prolactin is held down by dopamine. GH rises in deep sleep, fasting and stress. ACTH drives cortisol's daily rhythm (peak early morning).

  1. A. Melatonin
  2. B. Growth hormone
  3. C. Thyroxine
  4. D. ACTH

Answer: D. The circadian rhythm of cortisol follows the rhythm of ACTH release.

Thyroid physiology

Must-doRepeated15 past questions

T4 is made more, T3 is the active form (converted in tissues by deiodinase). Iodide trapping (sodium-iodide symporter), organification by thyroid peroxidase, coupling. Thyroid hormone is the main controller of basal metabolic rate. TSH is the best single test of thyroid status.

Answer: D. Thyroid hormones set the basal metabolic rate by raising cellular oxygen use.

Adrenal physiology

Must-doRepeated17 past questions

Zona glomerulosa: aldosterone (salt). Fasciculata: cortisol (sugar). Reticularis: androgens (sex). Medulla: adrenaline and noradrenaline. Cortisol from the fetus matures the lungs (surfactant); that is why mothers get steroids before preterm birth.

Pancreatic hormones

Must-doRepeated21 past questions

β cells make insulin (with C-peptide), α cells glucagon, δ cells somatostatin. Insulin moves glucose into muscle and fat through GLUT4. Oral glucose releases more insulin than IV glucose because of incretins (GLP-1, GIP).

Calcium homeostasis

Must-doRepeated14 past questions

PTH raises calcium: bone resorption, kidney reabsorption, and more activation of vitamin D (1α-hydroxylase). Calcitriol increases gut absorption. Calcitonin lowers calcium (minor role). Low magnesium blocks PTH release.

Cardiovascular Physiology 89 questions

Cardiac cycle: heart sounds, JVP, Wiggers diagram

Must-doRepeated28 past questions

S1 = closure of mitral and tricuspid valves, at the start of isovolumetric contraction. S2 = closure of aortic and pulmonary valves, at the start of isovolumetric relaxation. S3 = rapid ventricular filling (normal in the young, heart failure in adults). S4 = atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle. Splitting of S2 widens on inspiration.

Try a past question

Which phase of the cardiac cycle corresponds to the second heart sound (S2)?

  1. A. Isovolumetric contraction
  2. B. Isovolumetric relaxation
  3. C. Ventricular ejection
  4. D. Rapid ventricular filling

Answer: B. S2 is the closure of the semilunar valves, which starts isovolumetric relaxation.

Cardiac output

Must-doRepeated5 past questions

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume (about 5 L/min). Measured by the Fick principle or thermodilution. Venous return is driven by mean systemic filling pressure minus right atrial pressure; over time output equals venous return.

Try a past question

Which factor primarily determines venous return to the heart?

  1. A. Heart rate
  2. B. Mean systemic filling pressure
  3. C. Arterial blood pressure
  4. D. Pulmonary vascular resistance

Answer: B. Venous return depends on the gradient from mean systemic filling pressure to the right atrium.

Frank-Starling law

Must-do5 past questions

Frank-Starling law: the more the ventricle is filled (preload, end-diastolic volume), the stronger the next contraction, up to a limit. Bleeding lowers preload, so stroke volume and output fall. Contractility (inotropy) shifts the whole curve.

Try a past question

A person comes to ER with RTA history having thoracic and abdominal injury, found with hypovolemic shock. What could be the most probable reason in decrease cardiac output in this person?

  1. A. Decrease preload
  2. B. Decrease Stroke volume
  3. C. Increase preload
  4. D. decreased contractility of heart

Answer: A. Blood loss lowers venous return and preload, so stroke volume and cardiac output fall.

Pressure-volume loop

Must-doRepeated12 past questions

The pressure-volume loop: width = stroke volume; area = stroke work. More preload widens the loop to the right; more afterload makes it taller and narrower; more contractility moves the end-systolic point up and left. Aortic stenosis: high peak pressure; mitral regurgitation: no true isovolumetric phases.

ECG physiology

Must-do20 past questions

P wave: atrial depolarisation. PR interval: AV node delay (0.12-0.20 s). QRS: ventricular depolarisation. T wave: ventricular repolarisation. The plateau (phase 2) of the ventricular action potential comes from calcium entering through L-type channels; SA node pacemaker potential comes from funny (If) sodium current.

  3. C. Chloride influx
  4. D. Calcium influx through L-type channels

Answer: D. Calcium influx through L-type channels holds the plateau, phase 2.

Hemodynamics

Must-do25 past questions

Flow = pressure difference ÷ resistance. Poiseuille: resistance rises with the 4th power of a falling radius, so arterioles control resistance. Basal arteriolar tone is mainly sympathetic (α1). Laminar flow is silent; turbulence (high Reynolds number) causes murmurs and bruits.

Try a past question

Which factor primarily determines the basal tone of arterioles?

  1. A. Local metabolic factors
  2. B. Sympathetic nervous system
  3. C. Endothelial nitric oxide
  4. D. Renin-angiotensin system

Answer: B. Resting arteriolar tone is kept up mainly by sympathetic vasoconstrictor nerves.

Respiratory Physiology 72 questions

Lung volumes & capacities

Must-do13 past questions

Tidal volume about 500 mL; residual volume about 1.2 L (cannot be measured by spirometry; use helium dilution or body plethysmography). FRC = ERV + RV, where the lung's inward recoil balances the chest wall's outward recoil. Vital capacity = IRV + TV + ERV.

Try a past question

Which factor primarily determines the Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) of the lungs?

  1. A. Strength of inspiratory muscles
  2. B. Airway resistance
  3. C. Balance between lung and chest wall recoil
  4. D. Surfactant concentration

Answer: C. FRC is the volume where the inward pull of the lung equals the outward spring of the chest wall.

Pulmonary function tests

Must-do7 past questions

Obstructive disease: low FEV1/FVC, high TLC and RV (air trapping, auto-PEEP from incomplete emptying). Restrictive: normal or high FEV1/FVC, low TLC. DLCO falls in emphysema and fibrosis. Peak flow tracks asthma.

Try a past question

Which mechanism is primarily responsible for the phenomenon of auto-PEEP seen in obstructive lung disease?

  1. A. Increased lung compliance
  2. B. Reduced alveolar surface tension
  3. C. Increased inspiratory muscle strength
  4. D. Incomplete expiration before next inspiration

Answer: D. Narrowed airways do not let the lungs empty before the next breath, so air is trapped (auto-PEEP).

Gas exchange: diffusion, V/Q ratio, dead space, shunt

Must-doRepeated25 past questions

V/Q is highest at the lung apex and lowest at the base. Dead space (ventilated, not perfused): pulmonary embolism raises it. Shunt (perfused, not ventilated): does not improve with oxygen. Diffusion follows Fick's law; CO2 diffuses about 20 times faster than O2.

Try a past question

Which component of ventilation is increased in conditions with pulmonary embolism?

  1. A. Alveolar ventilation
  2. B. Physiological dead space
  3. C. Tidal volume
  4. D. Diffusion capacity

Answer: B. An embolus stops blood flow to ventilated alveoli, so physiological dead space rises.

Oxygen transport and the dissociation curve

Must-doRepeated20 past questions

Right shift (oxygen given up more easily): high CO2, acid, heat, 2,3-BPG, exercise. Left shift: fetal haemoglobin, carbon monoxide, alkalosis, cold, low 2,3-BPG. P50 is about 27 mmHg. Most CO2 travels as bicarbonate (about 70%), the rest on haemoglobin and dissolved.

Try a past question

Which factor most strongly shifts the oxygen- haemoglobin dissociation curve to the left?

  1. A. Increased pCO2
  2. B. Increased temperature
  3. C. Increased fetal haemoglobin
  4. D. Decreased pH

Answer: C. Fetal haemoglobin binds 2,3-BPG poorly, so it holds oxygen tighter: a left shift.

Regulation of respiration

Must-do10 past questions

Central chemoreceptors (medulla) respond to CO2 through CSF pH: the main drive. Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid, aortic bodies) respond mainly to low PO2. The fast rise in breathing at the start of exercise is neural (central command and limb receptors); chemical changes come later.

Try a past question

Which factor is most responsible for the rapid increase in ventilation at the ONSET of exercise?

  1. A. Increased arterial pCO2
  2. B. Increased arterial pO2
  3. C. Neural signals (central command from the motor cortex and muscle afferents)
  4. D. Increased lactic acid

Answer: C. Breathing rises at once when exercise starts, before blood gases change: a neural signal.

General Physiology 61 questions

Cell membrane transport

AskedINI-CET20 past questions

Simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion (carriers, channels), primary active transport (Na-K ATPase: 3 Na out, 2 K in), secondary active transport (SGLT, Na-glucose co-transport). Cystic fibrosis: the CFTR chloride channel is faulty.

Membrane potentials

AskedINI-CET17 past questions

The resting potential lies close to potassium's equilibrium potential because the resting membrane is most permeable to potassium. Nernst equation gives one ion's potential; Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz combines several. High blood potassium makes the resting potential less negative.

Try a past question

Which factor primarily determines the resting membrane potential of a neuron?

  1. A. Potassium permeability
  2. B. Sodium permeability
  3. C. Chloride permeability
  4. D. Calcium permeability

Answer: A. Potassium permeability is highest at rest, so it sets the resting potential.

Receptors, second messengers and signal transduction

Must-doRepeatedINI-CET21 past questions

G-protein receptors use second messengers (cAMP, IP3 and DAG, calcium). Receptor tyrosine kinases: insulin, growth factors. Nuclear receptors act as transcription factors: steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D, retinoids. Ion channel receptors: nicotinic, GABA-A.

Reproductive Physiology 42 questions

Menstrual cycle

Must-doRepeated8 past questions

Follicular phase: FSH grows the follicle, oestrogen rises; rising oestrogen switches to positive feedback and causes the LH surge, and ovulation follows about 36 hours later. Luteal phase (fixed about 14 days): progesterone from the corpus luteum, which raises body temperature.

  1. A. Progesterone

  3. C. LH
  4. D. FSH

Answer: A. Progesterone raises basal body temperature after ovulation.

Spermatogenesis

High-yield9 past questions

FSH acts on Sertoli cells (spermatogenesis, inhibin); LH acts on Leydig cells (testosterone). Spermatogenesis takes about 64-74 days and needs a scrotal temperature 2 °C below body temperature. Outside testosterone suppresses GnRH, LH and FSH, so the testes shrink and sperm counts fall.

  3. C. low GnRH, low LH, low FSH
  4. D. high GRH, high LH, high FSH

Answer: C. Injected testosterone feeds back to lower GnRH, LH and FSH.

Pregnancy hormones

High-yield11 past questions

hCG keeps the corpus luteum going until the placenta takes over progesterone production (about 8-10 weeks); it peaks around 10 weeks. Human placental lactogen raises maternal blood sugar (insulin resistance). Relaxin softens ligaments.

Lactation

High-yield10 past questions

Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin ejects it (let-down reflex from suckling). High oestrogen and progesterone in pregnancy block milk production; their fall after delivery lets it start. Suckling also suppresses ovulation (lactational amenorrhoea).

  1. A. Prolactin
  2. B. Oxytocin
  3. C. Estrogen
  4. D. Progesterone

Answer: B. Oxytocin contracts the myoepithelial cells, ejecting milk; prolactin only makes it.

Hematology 28 questions

RBC physiology

Must-doRepeated16 past questions

Erythropoietin is made by kidney peritubular cells when hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) rises. Red cell life about 120 days; destroyed in the spleen. Iron is absorbed in the duodenum (ferrous form) and controlled by hepcidin.

Hemostasis

Must-doRepeated4 past questions

Steps: vasoconstriction, platelet plug (adhesion through von Willebrand factor and GPIb, aggregation through GPIIb/IIIa), coagulation cascade, then fibrinolysis by plasmin. Vitamin K-dependent factors: II, VII, IX, X (and proteins C and S).

Blood groups

Must-doRepeated8 past questions

ABO antibodies are naturally IgM. O negative: universal red cell donor; AB positive: universal recipient. Bombay blood group (Oh) has no H antigen, so it reacts with all ordinary blood including O; only Bombay blood can be given. Rh antibodies are IgG and cross the placenta.

Gastrointestinal Physiology 31 questions

Gastric secretion

High-yieldRepeated8 past questions

Phases: cephalic (vagus, about 30%), gastric (gastrin, the biggest), intestinal. Parietal cells make acid and intrinsic factor; chief cells pepsinogen; G cells gastrin. Histamine, acetylcholine and gastrin all stimulate acid; somatostatin inhibits it.

Pancreatic secretion

High-yieldRepeated4 past questions

Secretin (from duodenal S cells, triggered by acid) brings bicarbonate-rich fluid. Cholecystokinin (I cells, triggered by fat and protein) brings enzymes and contracts the gallbladder. Trypsinogen is activated by enterokinase.

Bile

High-yield4 past questions

Bile acids are made from cholesterol; about 95% are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum (enterohepatic circulation). CCK contracts the gallbladder and relaxes the sphincter of Oddi. Ileal disease or resection leads to fat malabsorption and gallstones.

Motility

High-yield10 past questions

Liquids leave the stomach according to volume (pressure gradient); solids more slowly; fat slows emptying most (CCK). The migrating motor complex sweeps the fasting gut (motilin). Peristalsis is coordinated by the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus.

Muscle Physiology 44 questions

Skeletal muscle

AskedRepeatedINI-CET26 past questions

Calcium binds troponin C, moving tropomyosin off actin's binding sites. ATP is needed to release myosin from actin (no ATP = rigor mortis). Force is greatest at the optimum sarcomere length where actin and myosin overlap best. Smooth muscle uses calmodulin instead of troponin.

  3. C. Tropomyosin
  4. D. Calbindin

Answer: A. In smooth muscle calcium binds calmodulin (there is no troponin).

Cardiac muscle

AskedINI-CET16 past questions

Cardiac muscle cannot be tetanised because of its long refractory period. Cells are joined by intercalated discs with gap junctions (a functional syncytium). Calcium-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Strength rises with stretch (Frank-Starling).
